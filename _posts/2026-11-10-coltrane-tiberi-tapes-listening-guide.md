Four LPs of unheard John Coltrane can sound like a buying decision before they become a listening decision. With *The Tiberi Tapes*, I would start with a smaller question. Do two performances of the same tune make me want to hear what this band does next?

Released November 6, 2026, the standard collection comes as a four-LP set, three CDs, or a digital edition.[1] Its eleven selections include two versions of "Giant Steps," which give a first session a clear purpose without requiring an evening devoted to the whole archive.[2]

This is a listening guide built from the release documentation and independent reviews, not a hands-on assessment of the pressing. The distinction matters here because the reported limitations belong partly to the original recordings. Buying vinyl cannot answer whether those limitations will bother you.

## Know which Tiberi collection you are considering

The standard November release is a selection, not the entire surviving archive. A separate edition called *The Tiberi Tapes: The Complete Recordings* is scheduled to ship in early 2027. Its complete audio archive is on a USB drive, accompanied by an LP and a hardback book.[1] Do not read "complete" and assume the whole archive has been spread across more vinyl sides.

The story behind the recordings explains their unusual character. Jazzwise reports that saxophonist Frank Tiberi began taking a portable reel-to-reel recorder to Coltrane's club performances in Philadelphia and New York in 1960. He wanted material to study and transcribe, rather than recordings to sell.[2]

That gives me a useful way to approach the release. I would listen for a musician working through ideas in front of an audience, rather than expect a concert captured with a balanced professional recording setup. Tiberi's purpose also suggests a manageable task for the listener. Return to a passage, then hear what changes on the next performance.

## Begin with the two versions of Giant Steps

The opening performances are identified as "Giant Steps" at 226 and 262 beats per minute.[2] The difference supplies an immediate comparison, but speed need not become the verdict. I would ask how the group uses the tune at each tempo, and whether one version makes the exchanges between musicians easier to follow.

There is a reason this composition rewards that attention. In his essay hosted by the Library of Congress, saxophonist and author Carl Woideck explains that "Giant Steps" moves through a demanding sequence of chords linking three major keys. He describes it as a high point in Coltrane's pursuit of harmonic complexity.[3] You do not need to name those chords to hear the recurring melody and follow the improvisation that grows around it.

Woideck dates the studio album's recording to 1959 and release to 1960.[3] If you know that recording, use it as a reference rather than a test the live versions must pass. Notice where you recognize the tune, where your attention wanders, and which musician brings you back.

Adam Blyweiss's November 2 review for Treble offers a more specific lead. He draws attention to McCoy Tyner's expanded piano contribution in one version and Coltrane's prominence in the other.[4] That is a question to investigate on a second listen, not a substitute for making your own comparison.

## Follow someone other than the leader

A release organized around Coltrane's name can encourage me to treat every other instrument as background. I would resist that here. Treble's review discusses the changing personnel across the recordings, including drummer Pete La Roca and bassists Steve Davis and Reggie Workman, as well as musicians associated with the classic quartet.[4] This is not one fixed band documented on one night.

Choose one instrument for a repeat listen. Follow the piano when the saxophone pauses, or the drums as the solo continues. You can note a response or a change in density without identifying the player by ear.

For a longer investigation, Blyweiss singles out "Good Bait." He describes the performance moving away from bebop into something more abstract around its seventh minute, with Elvin Jones and Coltrane exchanging ideas later on.[4] I would use that observation as a listening prompt. Does the change feel abrupt, or can you hear the group preparing it?

That makes the longer performances less of an endurance exercise. You have a specific musical event to follow, and you can return to the complete track once the passage has made you curious.

## Accept the recording limits before choosing vinyl

The caveat is substantial. Jazzwise describes inconsistent audio in which saxophone and drums can be more distinct than bass and the lower piano register.[2] Treble's review also reports limited low frequencies, audience noise, and abrupt starts and stops.[4] These are reasons to sample the music before committing to a physical set.

The independent responses are still interested in the performances. In his November 1 All About Jazz review, Mike Jurkovic describes a challenging listen and treats Tiberi's audible reactions and audience appreciation as part of the experience.[5] A listener can value that proximity while another finds the same interruptions distracting.

Neither reaction needs correcting. If you want a clearly separated piano and bass image for a relaxed evening, these reports should temper your expectations. If you want to follow Coltrane's developing live ideas, the flaws may be a tradeoff you accept.

Keep that decision separate from pressing quality. A noisy source does not prove an LP has noisy surfaces. Equally, a quiet pressing cannot restore a bass part the original microphone failed to capture clearly. I have not inspected or played a physical copy, so I would not promise either result.

## Make the first session small enough to finish

I would begin digitally through an authorized edition, even if vinyl is the eventual goal. That lets the recording itself determine whether I want the box, rather than letting the box determine how much time I owe the music.

1. Play the first "Giant Steps" without reading a review. Keep the volume comfortable and note which instrument you can follow most easily.
2. Play the second version. Compare how the tempo affects your ability to follow the tune, rather than deciding that faster means better.
3. Return to a short passage with the piano or drums in mind. Check the reviewer's observation against what you hear.
4. Try "Good Bait" if the comparison makes you curious. Listen through the passage Treble identifies, then decide whether to continue with the full performance.[4]
5. Write one concrete note before stopping. "Return to the piano solo" or "the bass is too hard to follow for me" gives the next session a purpose.

There is no need to finish all eleven selections to make progress. If the opening comparison leaves you cold, try another Coltrane recording before treating a large archival set as an obligation.

## Buy for the performances you want to revisit

My reason to own this on vinyl would be wanting regular, deliberate sessions with these particular performances. The documentation alone would not settle that. Nor would an appealing package make the reported source balance suit my listening preferences.

The Journal's [Clifford Jordan listening guide](/blog/2026/10/clifford-jordan-let-it-ring/) offers another way into archival jazz through a different saxophonist and group. Keep that as a separate evening, rather than turn two discoveries into one long shopping list.

Start tonight with the two versions of "Giant Steps." If one sends you back to a piano passage or makes you want to follow the drums again, you have a reason to continue. Decide about four LPs after you know which performance you want to hear twice.
