The first song sounds open and clean. By the last chorus on the side, vocals spit, cymbals turn brittle, and busy passages seem to collapse into fuzz.

That pattern has a name: inner-groove distortion.

Some loss of tracking margin near the centre of a record is built into the format. Obvious distortion is not something you should accept on every LP. It may come from the cut, the pressing, groove wear, a dirty or worn stylus, incorrect tracking force, or poor cartridge alignment.

The useful question is not "Does vinyl do this?" It is "Does this record do it on every turntable, or does my turntable do it on many records?"

Here is how to find out without changing five settings at once.

## What inner-groove distortion sounds like

Inner-groove distortion gets worse as the stylus moves toward the label. Common signs include:

- harsh or splashy cymbals
- fuzzy vocals, especially on "s" sounds
- congested loud passages
- one channel sounding rougher than the other
- a clean first track followed by a distorted final track

A pop in one place is more likely dirt, damage, or a pressing defect. A steady rise in distortion across the side points more strongly toward tracking, alignment, or the way the record was cut.

Do not confuse the problem with ordinary surface noise. Dust and static tend to produce clicks and crackle. Inner-groove distortion changes the shape of the music itself.

## Why the last track is harder to play

A turntable spins at a constant number of revolutions per minute, but the groove does not pass the stylus at a constant linear speed.

Near the outer edge of a 12-inch record, one rotation covers much more groove than a rotation near the label. Breed Media gives a useful illustration for a 33 1/3 rpm LP: roughly 94 centimetres of groove pass the stylus per rotation at the outer edge, compared with about 47 centimetres near a 15-centimetre diameter. The available groove length per rotation has halved.

The inner groove still needs to carry the music. High frequencies and loud, complex passages become harder for the stylus to trace cleanly when the same musical information occupies less groove length.

There is another complication. Most home turntables use a pivoted tonearm. Its cartridge follows an arc across the record rather than staying perfectly tangent to the groove. Cartridge alignment decides where tracking error is lowest and how that error is spread across the side.

Cutting engineers work around these limits. They may lower the level, tame demanding high frequencies, change the running order, leave more dead wax, use 45 rpm, or split a long album across two records. That is why a well-cut final track can sound excellent while a crowded side exposes every weakness in a playback setup.

## Run the two-record test first

Before reaching for a screwdriver, compare records.

Choose two or three LPs that are clean and in good condition. Use records you know well, ideally from different labels or pressing plants. Play the final minute of each side at your normal volume.

The result narrows the problem quickly:

| What you hear | Most likely place to investigate |
| --- | --- |
| One record distorts in the same place every time | That copy, pressing, or cut |
| Many records distort near the label | Stylus condition, tracking force, alignment, or turntable limits |
| One channel distorts more across several records | Alignment, anti-skate, stylus wear, or cartridge setup |
| Distortion started suddenly | Dirt on the stylus, a setup change, or stylus damage |
| A second copy plays cleanly | Wear, damage, or a defect on the first copy |

If possible, play the suspect record on another correctly set up turntable. This is the cleanest way to separate the record from your equipment. A shop that sells turntables may help if you bought the record there.

## Clean the record and inspect the stylus

Start with the reversible jobs.

Clean the record using a record-safe method, then let it dry fully. Remove dust from the stylus with the cartridge maker's recommended tool and direction. Do not drag a brush from front to back against the cantilever, and do not use an unapproved liquid because it happened to be beside the turntable.

Look at the stylus under good light. A visible dust clump can make tracking fall apart. Damage and wear are harder to judge by eye, so use your playback log and the manufacturer's service guidance too.

If distortion appears across many records and the stylus has unknown hours, replacement may be safer than using valuable records as test material. A clean record cannot fix a worn diamond.

## Check tracking force against the cartridge specification

Tracking force is not a taste control. Set it inside the range specified for your cartridge, usually at the manufacturer's recommended value unless its instructions say otherwise.

Too little force can make the stylus lose contact with demanding groove modulations. That sounds rough and may cause mistracking. Adding extra force until the problem disappears is not a sound repair either. Excess force can increase wear and hide the actual setup fault.

Use a stylus-force gauge if you have one. A counterweight scale is useful, but a gauge checks what reaches the stylus. Confirm that the turntable is level before measuring.

If your turntable has fixed tracking force and a fixed cartridge, do not improvise with coins or other added weight. Clean the stylus, check the manual, and move to the comparison tests.

## Verify cartridge alignment with the right method

If the cartridge can move in the headshell, alignment matters.

Use the turntable manufacturer's overhang gauge, template, or specified alignment method. A generic protractor can work, but it must suit the arm geometry and be printed at exactly the right scale. Audio-Technica's alignment guidance makes the same core point: correct alignment reduces tracking error and distortion, and changing a cartridge means checking that position again.

When using a two-point protractor:

1. Switch off and unplug the turntable.
2. Protect the stylus until you are ready to measure.
3. Confirm the protractor printed at 100 percent scale.
4. Place the stylus on the first null point without letting the cartridge body touch the paper.
5. Square the cartridge to the grid.
6. Repeat at the second null point.
7. Adjust and recheck both points until neither correction ruins the other.
8. Tighten the mounting screws evenly, then check again.

Align the cantilever when the cartridge body is not a reliable visual reference. Work slowly. A slip near an exposed stylus costs more than patience.

After alignment, reset tracking force and anti-skate because moving the cartridge may change the balance.

## Treat anti-skate as a fine adjustment

Skating force pulls a pivoted tonearm inward as the record plays. Anti-skate applies an opposing force. A reasonable starting point is the turntable or cartridge maker's instruction, often a setting close to the tracking-force value on calibrated consumer decks.

Do not use anti-skate to compensate for a dirty stylus, wrong tracking force, or obvious alignment error. If one channel sounds consistently rougher, anti-skate belongs on the checklist, but it comes after the basic setup.

Avoid judging it with the smooth, ungrooved side of a record unless the turntable maker specifically recommends that method. A stylus behaves differently in a modulated groove.

## Know when the record is the problem

A perfect setup cannot repair groove damage or recut an overcrowded side.

Suspect the record when:

- distortion happens at the same musical moment on more than one turntable
- only one pressing or copy has the problem
- the inner tracks show visible groove wear
- the previous owner used damaged or poorly adjusted equipment
- other owners report the same issue for the exact release
- a replacement copy from the same cut behaves identically

Check the exact release on Discogs, including the matrix and runout, rather than assuming every edition shares the same cut. Community comments are not laboratory results, but a repeated complaint tied to one pressing is useful evidence.

A used record can look glossy and still have groove wear. Loud vocal peaks near the end of a side often reveal it first. Grade the playback you hear, not the shine you see.

## Decide whether a stylus upgrade makes sense

Stylus shape affects how well a cartridge traces fine groove detail. Ortofon describes elliptical styli as offering better tracking ability than spherical styli, while more advanced line-contact shapes are designed for higher tracking performance.

That does not mean buying the most expensive stylus will rescue every bad record. Compatibility, alignment, tonearm setup, cartridge condition, and the cut still matter. A finer profile also makes careful alignment more important.

Upgrade only after the current setup is clean, within specification, and aligned correctly. Otherwise you are paying to keep the same unresolved fault.

## Keep a short troubleshooting record

Inner-groove distortion is easy to misremember. You play the LP again months later and cannot recall whether the problem was this copy, the old stylus, or the turntable before its last setup.

Attach a short note to the exact pressing in My Vinyl+:

- side and track
- left, right, or both channels
- stylus and cartridge used
- cleaning date
- tracking force checked
- alignment checked
- result on a second turntable
- whether another copy played cleanly

Write what you heard, not a verdict you have not proved. "Side B final vocal sounds splashy on AT-VM95E; clean after realignment" is useful. "Bad pressing" after one play is not.

These notes also stop a damaged copy from being sold later as if nobody knew about the problem.

## A sensible order of checks

Use this sequence when the last track sounds wrong:

1. Replay the passage once at normal volume.
2. Compare two known-good records.
3. Clean the suspect record and stylus.
4. Check whether the problem affects one channel or both.
5. Verify the turntable is level.
6. Measure tracking force against the cartridge specification.
7. Check cartridge alignment with the correct gauge or protractor.
8. Set anti-skate according to the maker's guidance.
9. Try the record on another correctly set up turntable.
10. Record the result against the exact pressing.

Stop when the evidence points clearly to the record. More adjustment can make a good setup worse.

## The bottom line

Vinyl gives the stylus less groove length to work with near the centre of a side. That physical limit explains why final tracks are demanding. It does not excuse severe distortion across your collection.

Compare records before changing settings. Clean first. Check stylus condition, tracking force, alignment, and anti-skate in that order. Then test the exact copy elsewhere if you can.

One rough final track may be the record. Ten rough final tracks are asking you to inspect the turntable.
