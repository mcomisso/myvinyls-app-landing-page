You can track almost anything about a vinyl record. That does not mean you should track everything.

The useful question is simpler: which details will help you later?

For most collectors, the best record catalog is practical. It helps you identify the pressing, avoid duplicates, understand condition, find records, track value, and remember why a copy matters.

## The Essential Details

At minimum, every record should have:

- artist
- album title
- format
- label
- country
- release year
- catalog number
- barcode, if present
- pressing or edition

Most of this can come from Discogs when you add a release through My Vinyl+. That saves time and reduces the need to type data by hand.

The catalog number matters more than many beginners expect. It is often the quickest way to separate one pressing from another, especially when several editions share the same artwork.

## Condition

Condition is useful because it affects value, selling decisions, and upgrade choices.

You do not need to grade like a professional seller for every record, but you should know whether a copy is clean, noisy, damaged, incomplete, or worth replacing.

Helpful notes include:

- sleeve condition
- media condition
- warps
- scratches
- seam splits
- missing inserts
- noisy side or track
- cleaning needed

If you eventually sell or insure the collection, these notes become much more valuable.

## Purchase Details

Purchase details are optional, but they can be surprisingly useful.

Consider tracking:

- purchase price
- shop or seller
- date bought
- whether it was new or used
- why you bought it

This is not only about money. Sometimes the story is the point. A record bought at a favourite shop, a gig, a trip, or a small local fair can be worth remembering beyond its market value.

## Value and Upgrade Notes

Collection value can help you understand the size and shape of your collection, but it should not turn every record into a stock ticker.

Use value tracking for practical decisions:

- insurance
- selling duplicates
- deciding whether to upgrade
- spotting unusually valuable records
- understanding how much is tied up in the collection

Upgrade notes are also useful. If a copy is scratched, noisy, or missing an insert, mark it. Then, when you see another copy in a shop, you know whether it is worth considering.

## Storage Location

Storage becomes important as soon as the collection is no longer one neat shelf.

If you keep records in multiple rooms, crates, boxes, or storage units, track location in a way you will understand later. It does not need to be complicated.

Simple labels work:

- Living room shelf
- Office cube 3
- For sale crate
- Storage box A
- Cleaned, waiting to shelve

A searchable app makes this much easier than remembering where everything went.

## Personal Notes

Do not underestimate personal notes.

They can capture things database fields never will:

- favourite track
- who recommended it
- where you found it
- whether it sounds better than another pressing
- who borrowed it
- why it matters

This is where a catalog becomes a collection rather than inventory.

## The Bottom Line

The best vinyl tracking system is not the one with the most fields. It is the one you can keep using.

Track the details that help you identify, find, protect, enjoy, and make decisions about your records. Let Discogs data do the heavy lifting where it can.
