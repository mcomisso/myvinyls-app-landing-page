The most dangerous record listing is not always the obvious scam.

It is the listing that looks plausible enough. The seller has a few good photos. The price is high, but not absurd. The description uses the right collector words. The record is rare enough that you feel pressure to move before someone else does.

That is exactly when you need a process.

Counterfeit vinyl is not a theoretical problem. The UK Intellectual Property Office warned collectors in 2025 that fake versions of popular albums were being found on marketplaces, sometimes priced as high as GBP 1,000. In April 2026, City of London Police and the BPI disrupted a counterfeit vinyl operation in Luton, seizing 6,498 records with an estimated retail loss of GBP 259,920.

Most collectors will never knowingly buy from a fake pressing operation. The problem is that online listings blur together. Counterfeits, bootlegs, overgraded copies, wrong pressings, wishful prices, and ordinary sloppy listings can all look similar when you are scrolling at night.

Before you buy an expensive record online, run the listing through this checklist.

## Start With the Release, Not the Price

Do not begin by asking whether the price feels fair.

Start by asking whether the listing is actually for the release you think it is.

For valuable records, small details matter:

- exact label and catalogue number
- country of release
- pressing plant or mastering marks
- matrix and runout information
- barcode or lack of barcode
- sleeve variation
- insert, poster, obi, hype sticker, or booklet
- mono, stereo, coloured vinyl, test pressing, promo, or limited edition status

A seller can describe a record as "first press" because it sounds good in a listing. That does not make it true. Match the listing to a specific release page, then compare the seller's photos against that release rather than against your memory of the album.

Discogs is useful here because its release pages separate versions that casual listings often collapse into one phrase. My Vinyl+ helps once you have identified the right version: save the release details, condition, value notes, and wantlist context so the decision is not trapped in a browser tab you will forget later.

## Ask for the Photos That Prove the Claim

Generic photos are fine for a cheap new reissue. They are not enough for a rare or expensive record.

For a serious listing, you want photos of the actual copy:

- front cover
- back cover
- spine
- labels on both sides
- vinyl surface under angled light
- runout or dead wax area
- inner sleeve and inserts
- close-ups of damage, writing, splits, stains, stickers, and corner wear

The runout photo matters more than many beginners expect. Discogs uses runouts, barcodes, catalogue numbers, label details, and other identifiers to distinguish releases. If the seller is asking premium money for a specific pressing, they should be able to show the details that support that claim.

Blurry photos do not always mean dishonesty. Sometimes they just mean a seller is careless. But carelessness still matters when the price is high. If the listing depends on a detail you cannot see, ask for a clear photo before paying.

## Read the Description Like a Condition Report

An expensive listing should answer boring questions clearly.

Look for condition details on both the record and sleeve. "VG+" can mean a lot of things in real listings, especially when the seller is optimistic. You want to know what the seller actually saw:

- hairlines, scuffs, scratches, warps, dish warps, or edge warps
- surface noise, skips, repeating clicks, or a play grade
- seam splits, ring wear, corner bumps, writing, sticker residue, stains, or water damage
- missing inserts, non-original inner sleeves, or replaced parts
- whether the record has been cleaned

Be careful with descriptions that are all vibe and no evidence. "Mega rare", "holy grail", "museum copy", "investment piece", and "will only go up" are not condition notes. They are sales language.

The best seller notes are usually plain. "Record visually VG+ with light sleeve removal marks; plays with occasional low crackle; sleeve has 2 cm top seam split and price sticker residue." That is much more useful than "amazing copy".

## Watch for Counterfeit and Bootleg Signals

Not every unofficial record is pretending to be official. Some bootlegs are obvious to the buyer. The risky listings are the ones presented as legitimate pressings.

The IPO's collector guidance points to several useful warning signs: missing barcode or catalogue number where one should exist, poor-quality artwork, suspicious labels, odd runout information, missing inner sleeves, pitted vinyl, and sellers offering multiple copies of rare out-of-print records.

Use those signals as a group, not as one magic test. A vintage record may have no barcode because it was made before barcodes were normal. A worn sleeve may look rough because it is old, not fake. But a supposedly scarce sealed record with blurry artwork, vague provenance, no runout photos, and a seller who has several identical copies should slow you down.

Also separate three ideas:

**Counterfeit** means a fake copy trying to pass as the official release.

**Pirate** usually means officially released music pressed without permission.

**Bootleg** usually means unauthorised material such as live recordings, demos, or outtakes.

Collectors sometimes use those words loosely, but the buying decision is different. A bootleg sold honestly as an unofficial bootleg is one thing. A counterfeit sold as a valuable original is another.

## Check the Seller Before You Check Out

The seller matters as much as the record.

Discogs advises buyers to use safe marketplace habits because scammers target music platforms. Before buying, check:

- feedback score and the pattern behind it
- how long the account has been active
- whether feedback is recent
- whether the seller normally sells records like this
- whether several rare items appeared at once
- whether the seller communicates clearly
- whether they push payment outside the marketplace
- whether shipping, returns, and grading policies are clear

One negative review does not automatically kill a seller. A pattern does. Repeated complaints about overgrading, slow refunds, missing tracking, or "wrong pressing sent" are more important than one angry outlier.

Be especially careful when a seller tries to move the transaction somewhere with weaker buyer protection. A good deal is not good if the only reason it exists is that you have no practical way to recover your money.

## Compare Sold Prices, Not Dream Prices

Rare records are full of fantasy pricing.

The copy sitting unsold for GBP 400 does not prove the record is worth GBP 400. It proves someone would like GBP 400.

Before buying, compare:

- recent sold prices
- condition of those sold copies
- whether the same pressing sold, not just the same album
- whether complete copies sold for more than incomplete copies
- shipping and import costs
- currency conversion
- whether the latest sale was an outlier

Then decide your ceiling before messaging the seller. If you negotiate after you are emotionally committed, you will often negotiate against yourself.

This is where a collection app helps keep the hobby sane. In My Vinyl+, you can keep notes on what you are willing to pay, whether you already own another edition, and whether the record is a true upgrade or just a panic buy.

## Use a Three-Question Buy Rule

When the listing looks tempting, ask three questions:

1. Can I prove which pressing this is from the photos and identifiers?
2. Can I live with the worst condition issue mentioned or visible?
3. Would I still buy it tomorrow if the listing did not say "rare"?

If the answer to any of those is no, slow down. Ask for more evidence, make a lower offer, or walk away.

For very expensive records, add a fourth question: what would need to be true for me to resell this honestly? If you would struggle to describe the pressing, provenance, or condition clearly to someone else, you may not understand the listing well enough to buy it.

## Keep a Record of the Decision

If you buy the record, save more than the title.

Keep:

- purchase date
- seller or shop
- amount paid
- claimed pressing
- condition at purchase
- photos or notes from the listing
- why you bought this copy
- any questions to re-check when it arrives

When the record lands, compare it against the listing while the transaction is still fresh. Check the runouts, sleeve, inserts, vinyl surface, and playback. If something is wrong, clear notes make the conversation easier.

If everything is right, those notes still help. You now know exactly what you own, why you paid what you paid, and what would matter if you ever insure, trade, sell, or upgrade it.

## The Bottom Line

Expensive vinyl rewards patience.

A good listing should make the record easier to verify, not harder. Match the release first. Ask for the photos that prove the claim. Treat condition notes seriously. Watch for counterfeit signals. Check the seller. Compare sold prices. Set your ceiling before the fear of missing out takes over.

The goal is not to make buying rare records joyless. It is to keep the joy attached to the record, not to the rush.

If a listing survives the checklist, buy with more confidence. If it does not, let someone else take the risk.
