On RÓIS's *MO LÉAN*, bells and a mock radio announcement about death notices sit between the longer songs.[1] I would leave those short tracks in place on a first listen. They give you a way into an album about mourning that also makes room for humor and pleasure.

Domino's remastered reissue arrived on November 20, 2026, following the album's independent release in 2024.[2] This is a guide to approaching the music through its sequence and published criticism, not a review of the new pressing. Start with one question rather than a genre label. What changes when a singer's voice becomes a cry, a repeated phrase, or part of an electronic arrangement?

## Know what keening means here

The Fermanagh-born musician behind RÓIS is Rose Connolly. The Guardian's 2024 review connects this music with caoineadh, or keening, a tradition of vocal lament associated with mourning.[1] That context matters, but it need not turn your first listen into a history assignment.

In Deirdre Anna Rose Kelly's October 2026 Quietus interview, Connolly discusses the pressure to keep emotion within accepted public limits. Her account includes crying, screaming, and the social encounters that happen around a wake.[3] I would use that interview to resist the assumption that an album about death must remain quiet and solemn throughout.

Nor would I treat this as an untouched historical document. The Guardian describes synthesis, distortion, and drones, while Peter McGoran's November Hot Press review notes looped vocals and both English and Irish lyrics.[1][5] You are approaching a contemporary musician's work with inherited material, not a recording that lets you hear a past ritual exactly as it happened.

## Begin with the voice in "WHAT DO YOU SAY"

The Guardian describes the opener's mournful bass guitar and initially tentative chanting, with the voice processed through a granulator before building in intensity.[1] Christian Wethered's January 2025 KLOF Mag review focuses on the stuttering repetition of the title phrase.[4] Those observations suggest a more useful starting point than trying to catch every word immediately.

Follow the repeated phrase and ask when it stops sounding like an ordinary question. Does the processing make you attend to the syllables, the rhythm, or the effort in the voice? You do not need to identify the equipment behind every sound to notice a change in how the phrase works.

I would listen once without reading along, then return to a passage that was hard to place. The purpose is not to prove that a processed voice is less expressive than an unprocessed one. It is to notice what this treatment lets the performance do.

## Follow "CITÍ" into "CAOINE"

There are short "ANGELUS" tracks between the opening songs, rather than one uninterrupted run of full-length pieces.[6] Leave them in the sequence. The Guardian identifies the bells with the broadcast Angelus and describes them as part of the album's structure.[1]

For "CITÍ," that review points to an opening of sighs and a steady pulse, followed by the brighter sound of a taishogoto, a Japanese instrument.[1] I would follow the relationship between that pulse and the voice. When the accompaniment changes, ask whether it changes your sense of the same vocal sound.

"CAOINE" offers a different point of comparison. The Guardian describes its movement from a guttural opening toward electronic pop, while Wethered draws attention to its bass-heavy synth sound.[1][4] Listen through that change rather than treating the opening as an isolated display of vocal intensity. The arrangement gives you something else to follow once the initial shock has passed.

On a second pass, compare the beginnings of "CITÍ" and "CAOINE." Both reviews give you concrete details to test against what you hear. You can disagree with a critic's language and still use the description to find a passage worth replaying.

## Leave room for the radio joke

On the LP, "ANGELUS 3" opens side B. "OH LOVELY APPEARANCE OF DEATH" follows, then "THE DEATH NOTICES" and "FEEL LOVE."[6] That order is worth knowing before you assume the spoken track is an unrelated extra.

The Quietus interview describes the death-notices announcement as a joke about having no deaths to report. It also discusses "FEEL LOVE" as an ecstatic response voiced from beyond the grave.[3] Wethered hears the closing song as electronic pop pulled between bleakness and jubilant escape.[4] Neither description suggests an album that settles into one respectful mood and stays there.

I would let the announcement finish without reaching for the skip button. Then notice what the entrance of "FEEL LOVE" does to the ending. You do not have to find the joke comforting, but hearing it in place gives you more to consider than a playlist of the longer songs would.

## Try this first-listen checklist

Use a copy or authorized digital version you can access before deciding whether the LP belongs on your shelf. Here is the session I would set up.

1. Play the nine-track sequence through "FEEL LOVE," with the "ANGELUS" interludes and "THE DEATH NOTICES" included.[6] Leave shuffle off.
2. During "WHAT DO YOU SAY," follow one repeated phrase as its treatment changes. Save any lyric lookup for afterward.
3. Compare the entrances of "CITÍ" and "CAOINE." Note one difference in the voice or accompaniment rather than trying to describe the whole production.
4. Let the spoken announcement run into "FEEL LOVE." Ask whether that transition changes your understanding of the album's treatment of grief.
5. Finish before replaying a favorite. Write down one passage you want to hear again and what you want to check on the return.

If following a repeated phrase is unfamiliar, the Journal's [guide to Om's Pilgrimage](/blog/2026/11/om-pilgrimage-listening-guide/) offers another listening exercise. Here, I would keep the attention on vocal repetition rather than follow every instrument at once.

## Check the LP and digital endings separately

Domino's exact LP page identifies the clear-vinyl edition as WIGLP585 and lists five tracks on side A and four on side B. Its physical sequence ends with "FEEL LOVE."[6] The label's announcement specifies that the additional "PORT NA H-EALA" belongs to the digital format.[2]

That distinction matters if you encounter a general album page showing ten tracks. The LP listing also specifies an MP3 and WAV download card, but a downloadable extra is not another song cut into the record.[6] Check the physical side listing before ordering, rather than infer the contents from an expanded digital player.

The remaster announcement does not establish that the new LP sounds better than an earlier version. I would keep that question separate from whether the music interests me. For now, leave the short tracks in, listen through "FEEL LOVE," and choose the passage you want to return to before choosing an edition.
