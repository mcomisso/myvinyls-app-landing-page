A reissue display can send me straight toward an album I already know. The more useful question is which unfamiliar record deserves the next uninterrupted listen.

Rhino's Rocktober 2026 schedule includes Tom Verlaine's early solo albums for October 16, Devo and Randy Newman titles for October 23, and the Chills' *Submarine Bells* for October 30.[1] Those dates make a manageable starting point for exploring the catalog rather than treating the entire campaign as a shopping list.

I would start with the four albums below. This is a guide to discovering their music, not a listening test of the new pressings. The release announcement establishes dates and formats. It does not establish how these copies sound on a turntable.

## Tom Verlaine's Dreamtime follows his work with Television

If Television is where your knowledge of Tom Verlaine ends, *Dreamtime* gives you a specific next album to try. Rhino's catalog retrospective identifies it as his second solo album, released in 1981, following his self-titled 1979 debut.[2] The October 16 Rocktober listing specifies a single clear-vinyl LP.[1]

Verlaine's solo career did not begin with a complete break from his band. Rhino notes that several songs on the debut were originally intended for Television. The debut also includes "Kingdom Come," later covered by David Bowie.[2] That makes the first two solo albums a sensible place to continue if you want to hear more of Verlaine's songwriting.

My entry point would be "Always," the *Dreamtime* track embedded in Rhino's retrospective.[2] Listen to the guitar parts as well as the vocal melody before deciding whether the album belongs on your shelf. Knowing the artist's reputation is not the same thing as wanting to replay his solo work.

There is no need to buy all four Verlaine titles in the campaign. Start with one album, then use the chronology to decide where to go next.

## Devo's Something for Everybody needs more than a quick preview

Devo's contribution to the October 23 schedule is *Something for Everybody*, listed as a black-vinyl LP with a blue seven-inch record.[1] The extra disc is a package detail, not my reason to recommend investigating the album.

The musical question is what you make of Devo returning to an album of new material in 2010 after a twenty-year gap. Marc Masters' contemporary Pitchfork review is helpful because it does not pretend the comeback is uniformly successful. He criticizes the opening stretch, then identifies "Mind Games" as the point where the melodies, beats, and synthesizers become more interesting.[3]

That suggests a fairer audition than hearing a fragment of the opener and stopping. Try "Mind Games," then return to the start and hear the album's progression. You may disagree with the review. The point is to test its specific argument with your own ears.

I would put this on the shortlist for someone curious about later Devo, not someone who simply wants the band's best-known songs. A mixed contemporary assessment is useful buying information. A reissue does not erase the reservations that listeners had when an album first appeared.

## Randy Newman's Good Old Boys asks you to hear the narrator

Rhino lists *Good Old Boys* in its October 23 batch as a single 180-gram black-vinyl LP in the Rhino Reserve series.[1] Before considering the format, understand what kind of listening the album asks for.

Winston Cook-Wilson's Pitchfork retrospective describes the 1974 record as an examination of Southern bigotry and pride through characters whose words are not straightforward statements of Newman's beliefs.[4] It contains explicit racist language, particularly in the opening song, "Rednecks." That is worth knowing before you play it for guests or introduce it to a younger listener.[4]

The review also explains the role of "Birmingham" and "Marie" in developing the central character's home life, and the album's combination of piano, orchestra, and a rock rhythm section.[4] This is not a record I would suggest judging by a chorus detached from its setting.

For an entry point, try "Marie," then listen to the complete album when you can follow the words. Ask who is speaking, what that person believes, and whether the arrangement supports or complicates the story. You do not have to enjoy the discomfort or buy the record to understand why its songwriting deserves attention.

## The Chills' Submarine Bells is the upcoming discovery pick

*Submarine Bells* is the one here with a release date still ahead of this article. Rhino schedules its Rhino Reserve edition for October 30 as a single 180-gram black-vinyl LP.[1] Check with your local shop before making a trip. A label's schedule is not a stock confirmation.

Rhino's album history places the Chills in New Zealand's alternative-pop scene and describes their move from Flying Nun to the Warner subsidiary Slash, which released *Submarine Bells* in 1990.[5] The album has twelve original songs, with Martin Phillipps as singer-songwriter and Gary Smith producing.[5]

Start with the opener, "Heavenly Pop Hit." Rhino's retrospective singles it out while describing the album's memorable melodies and atmospheric arrangements.[5] Treat that label assessment as an invitation to listen, not as an independent verdict on the new edition.

Of these four, this would be my first suggestion for someone seeking an unfamiliar band rather than another chapter from a familiar name. Hear the opening track, then give the remaining songs a chance before choosing a physical copy. October 30 is a reason to investigate this week, not a deadline for deciding that you love it.

## Audition the music before visiting the shop

Here is the listening check I would use before spending anything:

1. Choose the album that answers a real curiosity. That might be Verlaine after Television, later Devo, Newman's character writing, or the Chills' New Zealand pop.
2. Play the suggested entry track through an official artist or label channel, or a licensed service. Do not treat a short preview as the whole argument for an album.
3. Hear the full album before ordering if you can. For Newman, pay attention to the narrators. For Devo, test whether the change identified in the contemporary review matters to you.
4. Write down one concrete reason you would replay it. A song title or arrangement you want to hear again is more useful than "important album."
5. Confirm the exact title, format, release date, and local availability with the shop. Rhino says this campaign is offered through select physical retailers while supplies last.[1]

If buying has overtaken listening lately, use the [guide to building a vinyl listening queue](/blog/2026/08/build-vinyl-listening-queue/) to make room for the records already waiting at home.

## Leave with one musical reason

My aim with a reissue month is to find an album I will play again. I would rather discover one unfamiliar record than complete a row of campaign stickers.

Pick an entry track tonight. If it holds your attention, listen to the album and ask the shop about that specific edition. If it does not, leave it. The useful result is knowing which music you want to return to, whether or not a new record comes home.
