A song called "Air" ought to be reassuring. On Talking Heads' *Fear of Music*, even breathing becomes a subject for suspicion. Jayson Greene's Pitchfork review finds humor in that habit of making ordinary things, including paper and animals, sound difficult to trust.[2] I would approach it on those terms, rather than hear it only as preparation for *Remain in Light*.

Rhino's expanded editions of the 1979 album are scheduled for today, November 13, 2026. The vinyl choices separate the remastered album and rarities across two LPs, or add two 1979 concerts in a six-LP set.[1] I would start with the original songs rather than the size of the package.

This is a guide to exploring those songs using the published track lists and independent criticism. It is not a review of the new pressing. The question is whether the album's strange treatment of familiar subjects makes you want to hear it again.

## Start without translating I Zimbra

The opening track does not give you a conventional story to follow. Greene identifies its vocal material as nonsense syllables from the Dada poet Hugo Ball, with Robert Fripp adding guitar. He describes a busy arrangement of congas, funk guitar, and synthesizers whose motion does not produce an uncomplicated feeling of progress.[2]

I would listen first for repetition rather than meaning. Pick a short guitar figure or a percussion pattern and follow it while the voice enters. If you cannot identify every part, keep listening to the one you can recognize.

That changes the job of an opening song. You do not need to solve the words before deciding whether the rhythm interests you. After "I Zimbra," let "Mind" and "Paper" continue in their published order.[1] Notice what happens when ordinary language returns, rather than skipping straight to the best-known song.

## Let Cities turn a sensible question strange

"Cities" offers an easier subject to grasp, choosing somewhere to live. The Guardian's 2016 guide describes a narrator weighing places' attractions while becoming less composed. It also points to the recording's fade-in and fade-out, which make the song feel like an excerpt from a longer search.[3]

That gives a first listen a specific task. Follow the bass and drums through the comparisons, then ask whether the singer sounds closer to making a decision by the end. On a second pass, pay attention to the entrance and exit instead of only the central groove.

The recording context helps explain why the rhythm deserves that attention. The Guardian describes the band recording basic backing tracks at Tina Weymouth and Chris Frantz's loft, with cables running to a Record Plant mobile van outside.[3] It is useful background for hearing the band as an ensemble, rather than treating the album as David Byrne's words with accompaniment.

I would leave "Cities" beside "Life During Wartime" and "Memories Can't Wait," the next two songs in the original sequence.[1] The practical question about where to live then sits beside titles concerned with survival and memory. Hear the sequence before building a playlist of isolated favorites.

## Give side two's ordinary subjects equal attention

The original album's second side runs through "Air," "Heaven," "Animals," "Electric Guitar," and "Drugs."[1] Those short titles make a useful listening checklist. They do not guarantee that the song will behave the way its subject suggests.

Greene reads "Air" and "Animals" as part of the album's comic suspicion of everyday existence. His account of "Heaven" offers a different possibility, a wish for order and release from constant observation.[2] I would listen for whether that song feels restful in this company, or whether repetition becomes unsettling too. There is no need to settle on one reading immediately.

"Electric Guitar" deserves attention even if you normally wait for solos. In Guitar.com's retrospective, Andy Price points out that the song refuses the obvious instrumental celebration its title might promise. He describes a small, nervous riff rather than a grand lead break.[4]

Use that as a prompt to notice what the guitar does between vocal phrases. Does it answer the singer, interrupt, or repeat? A short contribution can matter without becoming a solo, and this is a useful album on which to practice hearing that distinction.

## Keep the original album separate from the extras

Rhino's two-LP edition does not spread the original album over four playable sides. The eleven-song album occupies the first record, the six rarities occupy one side of the second record, and the remaining side has an etching.[1] That matters if you want to hear the original album without bonus tracks changing its ending.

The Second Disc's catalog account also makes a useful distinction between rare and newly released. It traces the studio extras to earlier expanded editions, a digital rarities collection, and the 1980 promotional twelve-inch version of "I Zimbra."[5] Do not assume every extra is a newly discovered recording.

For a first session, stop after "Drugs." On another day, choose one alternate version of a song you already know. I would compare "Cities" with its alternate version before playing every bonus track. That gives the comparison a familiar reference without turning discovery into an inventory exercise.

## Compare one song with its 1979 stage versions

The six-LP set adds concerts recorded at Boston's Berklee Performance Center on August 24, 1979, and the Capitol Theatre in Passaic on November 17, 1979. Both include "Cities," "Heaven," and "Life During Wartime," so there are several ways to compare the same composition.[1]

Some performances have appeared before. The Second Disc identifies material previously released on *The Name of This Band Is Talking Heads* and its expanded edition.[5] The attraction is the concert context, not a promise that every minute is unheard.

If you know "Life During Wartime" through *Stop Making Sense*, keep the dates in mind. The Guardian discusses that film's performance as an expanded-band interpretation captured in 1983.[3] The concerts in this reissue document 1979, not the film's tour. I would compare the studio recording with one of those earlier performances before bringing the later version into the session.

## Try an album session, then one comparison

Here is the order I would use, whether starting with an existing LP or an authorized digital edition. Keep the album listen and the version comparison separate.

1. Play the original eleven tracks in order. For vinyl, turn the first record over after "Memories Can't Wait" and continue with "Air."[1]
2. During "I Zimbra," follow one repeating instrumental part. Let understanding the arrangement come before interpreting the vocal.
3. On "Cities," listen for the rhythm beneath the narrator's search. Keep listening through the fade rather than stopping at the last clear phrase.
4. Give "Heaven" and "Electric Guitar" the same attention as "Life During Wartime." Note which song makes you curious enough to return.
5. End the original album at "Drugs." Later, compare your chosen song with an alternate take or a 1979 concert version, not both at once.

For a different record to explore afterward, the Journal's [Rocktober listening guide](/blog/2026/10/rocktober-2026-listening-guide/) offers other recent catalog starting points. There is no need to make today's session cover them too.

Begin with the ordinary subjects on the sleeve and hear what the band does to them. After the album ends, choose one song whose meaning or arrangement still puzzles you. Make that the next listen, before deciding whether you need any larger edition.
