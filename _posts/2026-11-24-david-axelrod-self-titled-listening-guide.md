David Axelrod's self-titled album is an awkward record to date by ear. Its history gives you a better starting point. Old rhythm tracks became the basis of a new album completed decades later, with Axelrod returning to music he had left unfinished.[3] I would listen for what he changed, rather than treat it as an untouched session finally escaping a vault.

Now-Again has announced a vinyl reissue of the 2001 album for Record Store Day Black Friday, whose 2026 event falls on November 27.[1][2] That makes this a useful week to hear the existing album before deciding whether to buy it. The question is whether its combination of rhythm section and orchestration holds your attention beyond the promise of a rediscovered recording.

## Understand what was unfinished

The project began as a planned Electric Prunes album based on Faust. Axelrod recorded the rhythm parts, but the album did not reach its intended finished form. In his August 2001 interview with Sound On Sound's Sam Inglis, he described recovering the music on an acetate and writing new arrangements after the original scores had disappeared.[3]

That changes what the word archival means here. This is not simply a performance preserved at one moment. Axelrod used the recovered material as something he could compose around again. The original idea and the released album belong to different stages of his career.

I would avoid assigning the entire record one late-1960s session date. The current label account dates the lost project to 1969, while the contemporary Sound On Sound interview places the rhythm recordings in 1968.[2][3] The disagreement does not need a guess. What matters for this listen is the documented sequence of unfinished rhythm tracks, recovery, and later completion.

## Follow the bass before the orchestra

Start with an instrumental such as "Jimmy T." Joe Tangari's January 2002 Pitchfork review describes Howard Roberts' guitar opening, a rhythm section with Carole Kaye and Earl Palmer, a baritone saxophone solo, and dissonant strings.[4] Those are useful parts to listen for individually, rather than file the whole sound under orchestral soul and move on.

On a first pass, keep your attention on the bass and drums when other instruments enter. Does the rhythm feel like the main event, or does an arriving melody change where you listen? On a second pass, follow the strings instead. You do not need to identify which note came from which session to notice how the parts interact.

Axelrod told Inglis that he often interrupted melodic phrases with a short drum break or bass fill. He said he had become conscious of that habit only after others drew attention to it.[3] I would therefore listen for the moments when an arrangement thins out, as well as the moments when it fills up. A brief exposed rhythm part can explain more about the construction than the loudest passage.

## Let the vocal tracks set the sequence

The first and last tracks require a different approach. "The Little Children" has Ras Kass on vocals, while Lou Rawls sings "Loved Boy" at the end. Pitchfork identifies these as the album's two vocal songs and its newly recorded opening and closing pieces.[4]

In the Sound On Sound interview, Axelrod explained that he wanted to begin with "The Little Children" and end with the story of his son, the subject of "Loved Boy." He linked the closing song to the opening rap when discussing the album's sequence.[3] That is a stronger reason to play the record in order than a general rule about respecting albums.

I would resist starting with the most immediately appealing instrumental and stopping there. Hear the opener, let the instrumental stretch run, then leave enough attention for Rawls. The sequence gives you a way to judge the middle tracks as part of an album, rather than as isolated material that might interest a producer searching for a sample.

## Use one focused listening pass

You can try this with an existing authorized digital edition or a copy you already own. It does not require the forthcoming pressing. Keep the track list available, but leave the session history aside once playback starts.

1. Play "The Little Children" first. Notice the relationship between the voice and the orchestration before expecting a conventional band groove.
2. On "Jimmy T," follow the bass through the instrumental changes. Then return once and follow the strings instead.
3. Compare "Fantasy for Ralph" with "The Shadow Knows." Tangari describes a prominent soprano saxophone contribution in the former and a darker, rhythm-led character in the latter.[4] Decide whether those different approaches sustain your interest.
4. Continue to "Loved Boy" without substituting another track. Ask whether the ending changes how you understand the opener.
5. Write down one whole track you want to hear again. A title is more useful than a general note that you liked the production.

For another exercise in following a rhythm section through a longer form, the Journal's [guide to Fela Kuti's Expensive Shit](/blog/2026/10/fela-kuti-expensive-shit-listening-guide/) offers a different musical setting. Here, keep the focus on how orchestration changes your attention within comparatively short pieces.

## Read the criticism before buying the story

The recovered-session history is interesting, but it did not persuade every reviewer. Tangari praised the way the old recordings and new sessions fit together.[4] The Guardian's August 2001 review found the rhythm tracks dated and thought pieces including "Fantasy for Ralph" and "The Shadow Knows" still sounded unfinished.[5]

Those judgments point to a specific listening question. Does the space around the instrumental lines feel purposeful to you, or do you keep waiting for another part to arrive? There is no need to settle that by choosing the more enthusiastic review. Use the disagreement to identify what you should test with a full track.

Neither review assesses the forthcoming Now-Again pressing. Nor does the label's discussion of all-analog editions of Axelrod's earlier Capitol albums establish the mastering chain for this release.[2] I would not transfer a technical claim from one reissue program to another because the same artist and label appear on both.

## Decide whether you want the whole album

Now-Again's announcement describes a 12-page book with photographs from the 2001 sessions and an account of the album's history.[2] That may matter if you want to study how the project came together. It does not answer whether you will return to the music, and I have not auditioned the new pressing.

My buying test would be simple. After hearing the album in order, return to the track you noted. If the appeal disappears once you stop thinking about the recovery story, keep listening before adding a physical copy. If you want to follow the arrangement again, the album has given you a musical reason to investigate the reissue.

Start with "The Little Children" and stay through "Loved Boy." Then choose one instrumental to replay with your attention on the bass. That will tell you more about whether this record belongs on your shelf than trying to decide which decade it sounds like.
