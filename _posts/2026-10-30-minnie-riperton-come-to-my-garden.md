Recognizing "Les Fleurs" is not quite the same as knowing Minnie Riperton's *Come to My Garden*. An opening song can become so familiar that it keeps the rest of an album out of view. I would start this record with a different aim, to find the song I want to hear after the opener.

There is a reason to look now. A September 25 announcement scheduled Capitol's vinyl reissue for October 2, 2026.[1] That makes this month's edition a useful prompt to explore the album, without assuming a new pressing is necessary to appreciate it.

The most useful name to keep beside Riperton's is Charles Stepney. His arrangements give us something specific to investigate beyond a famous voice. This is a guide to that collaboration, not a review of the new vinyl's sound.

## Hear Les Fleurs as an arrangement

In his 2024 Guardian guide to Riperton's songs, Stevie Chick describes "Les Fleurs" moving from pizzicato strings into a much larger psychedelic orchestral sound.[2] That change in scale is a better starting point than listening only for the singer's highest notes.

On the first pass, notice where the performance begins to feel crowded or expansive. On a replay, ask what caused that impression. Was it more voices, a change in instrumental texture, or the way the arrangement framed Riperton? You do not need a technical term for every sound to distinguish those possibilities.

Keep the volume steady while you compare passages. The exercise is to hear what the arrangement changes, rather than make an already large passage louder. If one moment is familiar from elsewhere, let the track continue past it instead of treating recognition as the end of the listen.

A note such as "the voices behind her change the scale of the song" is useful. It gives you something to check on a replay. "Beautiful production" leaves much more of the work unexplained.

## Put Charles Stepney beside the singer

Stepney and Riperton had already worked together in Rotary Connection. Alexis Petridis's 2022 Guardian profile describes the group's attempt to combine blues, classical music, and psychedelic rock, with Stepney in charge of the project.[3] The orchestral ambition of her solo debut belongs in that history.

The profile also includes a revealing recollection from Stepney's daughter Charlene. Her mother wanted his contribution acknowledged on the album cover with a presentation credit, but he was uncomfortable with that prominence.[3] It is a reason to read the credits, not evidence that one collaborator mattered more than the other.

I would keep both names in a collection note. An artist field helps you find another Riperton album. A note about Stepney helps you pursue the arrangements that caught your attention, even when the singer changes.

Petridis discusses Stepney's work with Terry Callier, Marlena Shaw, and the Dells, among others.[3] Choose a follow-up by the particular credit that interests you. A shared arranger is a useful connection, but it does not promise that every record will sound like this one.

## Follow the songs beyond the opener

Capitol's current tracklist puts "Rainy Day in Centerville" at the end of side A. Side B begins with "Close Your Eyes and Remember" and ends with "Whenever, Wherever."[4] Those positions give you a route through the album that does not stop at its first track.

Chick singles out "Rainy Day in Centreville" for the way its strings and brass expand and then return to tension.[2] His article uses a different spelling of the place name from Capitol's listing. Here, I use the spelling on that listing so you can find the track without mistaking it for another song.

Listen for the return to a smaller or less settled passage as carefully as the expansion. Does that retreat change how you hear Riperton's next phrase? Treat the critic's description as a question to test, rather than an answer you must reproduce.

On side B, "Close Your Eyes and Remember" offers another point of attention. Chick discusses its childhood memories and images of nature.[2] Follow the words for one pass before returning to the instrumental setting. That gives the singer's subject as much attention as the size of the production.

For "Whenever, Wherever," Chick points to the contrast between Riperton's declarations and the backing singers' complex staccato parts.[2] Try following the backing voices through a passage. Then replay it with your attention on the lead. You are hearing different parts of the same performance, not ranking the musicians.

## Keep Perfect Angel separate

If "Lovin' You" is your reference point for Riperton, do not expect to find it on this album. It belongs to *Perfect Angel*, her later collaboration with Stevie Wonder. Marcus J. Moore's 2017 Pitchfork review describes that album's mixture of funk, reggae, folk, and soul in contrast to *Come to My Garden*'s jazz arrangements and operatic character.[5]

That distinction matters more than deciding which album is the definitive introduction. A listener drawn to the spare arrangement of "Lovin' You" may need time with the orchestration here. Someone drawn to Stepney's writing has a different reason to begin with the debut.

I would compare one song from each album rather than expect a whole career to fit one description. Notice whether the accompaniment leaves the voice exposed or surrounds it with other activity. Neither approach automatically makes a performance more expressive.

## Read the reissue details without buying the adjectives

The September announcement names October 2 as the release date. At the time of writing, Capitol's store instead displays "Ships on: November 4, 2026" for an order placed there.[1][4] An announced release date and a seller's current shipment estimate answer different questions. Check the latter before planning around a delivery.

Capitol credits Levi Seitz at Black Belt Mastering with mastering the edition for vinyl.[4] That identifies a person and studio. It does not, by itself, establish the source used or tell us how the finished pressing compares with another copy.

The same listing makes promotional claims about detail, warmth, and a definitive presentation.[4] I would not turn those adjectives into a listening verdict. I have not auditioned the pressing, and the music discussed above does not depend on those claims being true.

If you already have a playable copy, use it for this session. If you are discovering the album digitally, check the track order against the label's listing. The purchase decision can wait until you know which songs you want to return to.

## Find your next song tonight

Here is the session I would try:

1. Play "Les Fleurs" all the way through. Mark one change in the arrangement that you want to hear again.
2. Continue through side A rather than replaying the opener immediately. Give "Rainy Day in Centerville" your attention before the side ends.
3. Begin side B with "Close Your Eyes and Remember." Follow the words before concentrating on the accompaniment.
4. At "Whenever, Wherever," compare the lead and backing voices across two passes through a passage.
5. Choose one song beyond the opener for your next listen. Only then decide whether a physical edition fills a gap in your collection.

If following a production credit appeals to you, the Journal's [guide to hip-hop sampling and vinyl](/blog/2025/11/hip-hop-sampling-vinyl-producers-guide/) offers another route into discovering the people behind a familiar recording.

The point is not to make "Les Fleurs" less important. It is to stop recognition of one song from standing in for an album. Before putting this record away, choose the next track you want to cue and write down what you want to hear in it.
