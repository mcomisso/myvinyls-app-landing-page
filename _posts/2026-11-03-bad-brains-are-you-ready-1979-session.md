A familiar song title can hide an unfamiliar recording. That is the reason I would pause over Bad Brains' *Are You Ready*, rather than assume another appearance of "Pay to Cum" means another copy of a performance I already own.

*Are You Ready: The First Studio Recording* documents a March 1979 session at Inner Ear Studios in Arlington, Virginia. The six tracks arrive officially on November 6 through Trust Records, on vinyl, cassette, and digital formats.[1] This week's release offers a specific question to investigate. What changes when you hear the band before the recordings that established its reputation?

I would use the available preview to begin that comparison, then hear the complete session when it comes out. This is a guide to the recording's place in the catalog and a plan for listening, not a review of a physical pressing I have tested.

## Place the session before the familiar releases

The date is the useful part of the long subtitle. DC Public Library's release event description identifies the March 1979 recording as a six-song session captured in one hour, newly mixed by Ian MacKaye in cooperation with Bad Brains.[2] That gives the material a more precise identity than an unspecified collection of early demos.

It also separates this release from *Black Dots*. The announcement reproduced by Blabbermouth places the recordings later issued as *Black Dots* after this session, followed by the 1980 *Omega Sessions* material. The band's debut "Pay to Cum" seven-inch appeared in 1980, and the self-titled debut followed on cassette in 1982.[4]

For my own shelf notes, I would record both the session date and the edition's release year. A 2026 issue of a March 1979 performance is not a 1979 pressing. Nor does recognizing the song "Black Dots" tell you that you are hearing the same performance as the album with that title.

That distinction is the reason to investigate this release even if you know the songs. It is also a reason to check which recordings you already have before ordering. Familiar titles alone cannot answer that question.

## Listen for the players, not only the speed

Bad Brains' reputation can make an early recording sound like a test of how fast a band could play. Nabil Ayers' 2020 Pitchfork retrospective offers a broader starting point. It traces the musicians' interest in jazz fusion, including Return to Forever and Mahavishnu Orchestra, before their discovery of punk, and describes their early combination of speed and instrumental skill.[3]

The quartet consists of H.R. on vocals, Dr. Know on guitar, Darryl Jenifer on bass, and Earl Hudson on drums.[2] Keeping those roles in mind gives me something concrete to follow beyond the overall rush of a short song.

On a first pass, I would listen for where the guitar phrase stops and how the bass and drums carry the next entrance. On the next, I would follow the vocal phrasing against that rhythm. These are listening prompts, not claims that the unreleased tracks contain a particular break or arrangement.

There is a useful later comparison in *I Against I*. Ayers describes the 1986 album's move toward restraint and groove, with a stronger hard-rock and metal guitar presence.[3] Hearing an early session beside that album can test a narrow assumption about the band. Speed was part of its music, but it was not the only direction the musicians could take.

## Start with the preview, then hear all six tracks

Pitchfork's announcement includes the early studio version of "Pay to Cum." Its accompanying video uses CBGB footage from October 1979, so the images and the March studio audio are separate historical documents.[1] I would not use that video to make claims about how the studio session looked.

The published sequence continues with "Don't Bother Me," "Black Dots," "Migraine," "Send No Flowers," and "Jamming at the Atlantis."[1] Keep the sequence together on the first full listen rather than jumping straight to another version of the best-known title.

Here is the comparison I would make:

1. Play the official "Pay to Cum" preview through the link in the announcement. Listen through once without switching recordings.
2. Choose one later, clearly identified version of that song. Note its release before comparing it, so two different uploads do not masquerade as two different performances.
3. Compare the opening, vocal entrances, and ending. Write down an audible difference rather than deciding that the earlier version must be more authentic.
4. When the complete session is available, hear the six tracks in order. Ask whether a song other than "Pay to Cum" changes your interest in the release.
5. Return to the later recording. Decide which performance you want to replay, without requiring the historical discovery to become your favorite.

If that approach appeals, the Journal's [Rocktober listening guide](/blog/2026/10/rocktober-2026-listening-guide/) applies the same principle to other recent catalog releases. Start with a musical question before choosing a physical edition.

## Check the format before treating it as an album purchase

The release announcement specifies a 45 RPM twelve-inch record with an etched B-side and a double-sided archival-photo insert. It credits Ian MacKaye with the new mix and Pete Lyman at Infrasonic with mastering.[4] Those are production and package details, not evidence that the pressing sounds better than another format.

The etched B-side matters if you expect two playable sides from a twelve-inch sleeve. The six-song program is what you are buying, not an unmentioned second side of music. Check that arrangement and the speed with the shop before paying, especially if its listing shortens the full title.

I would choose vinyl for the physical presentation only after deciding that I want the session. The cassette and digital options establish that hearing the material does not require buying an LP.[1] There is no need to assign sonic superiority to a format before comparing actual copies.

## Follow the recording back to its local archive

This week's activity extends beyond the record's release. DC Public Library lists a conversation and listening event with Jenifer and MacKaye at Martin Luther King Jr. Memorial Library on November 5, from 6 to 8 p.m. The listing currently says the event is full and registration is required.[2] Check it before traveling rather than treating a mention here as an available seat.

The same page explains that the DC Punk Archive collects material about musicians and the places and activities around them, including venues, record stores, radio, art, and activism.[2] A session gains context when it sits beside evidence of the community that made it possible.

That community did not end with the famous early bands. Ron Knox's 2016 NPR report follows a later generation of DC hardcore musicians and the effort to introduce new bands through local shows.[5] It is historical reporting about that period, not a calendar for today's scene. I would read it alongside the release coverage to avoid mistaking a city's musical history for one fixed lineup of names.

## Decide what you want to hear again

The useful discovery is an identifiable performance, not the phrase "earliest known" on its own. A session can matter historically without becoming the version you prefer to play.

Start with the "Pay to Cum" preview today and identify the later version you want to compare. After November 6, give the full session one uninterrupted listen before choosing a format. The familiar title earns another place on the shelf only if this recording gives you a reason to return.
