An unheard jazz tape can get my attention before I know whether I enjoy the musician on it. With Clifford Jordan's *Let It Ring: Live at the Jazz Showcase*, I would reverse that order. Use the announcement to investigate the tenor saxophonist, then decide whether this particular recording belongs on the shelf.

The October 6 announcement schedules a 180-gram double LP for Record Store Day Black Friday on November 27, 2026, with CD and digital editions following on December 4.[1] That leaves time to hear some Jordan without treating an unfamiliar record as an urgent purchase.

There is a useful listening route here, through an available preview, an earlier studio album, and the history of the Chicago club. This is a guide to taking that route, not a review of a vinyl pressing I have heard.

## Start with the musicians, not the rarity

Resonance's official release page identifies Jordan on tenor saxophone, Al Haig on piano, Wilbur Ware on bass, and Kenny Clarke on drums. It dates the Jazz Showcase engagement to September 30 through October 4, 1976.[2] Those names tell me more about what to investigate than the phrase "previously unreleased."

The same page currently offers "Confirmation" as the advance track, while the complete digital album remains a preorder.[2] Start there if you want to hear this group rather than guess from its reputation. You do not need to order the album to make the first listening decision.

On a first pass, follow Jordan through the tune. On a second, choose one other instrument and keep your attention there, even when the saxophone returns. Ask whether you want to hear more of the group once you have stopped concentrating only on its leader.

That exercise does not require identifying every chord or counting choruses. I would make one plain note, such as "follow the bass next time," rather than try to write a verdict on the entire recording after one track.

## Put Glass Bead Games beside the new discovery

For a second point of reference, try Jordan's *Glass Bead Games*. In his 2007 All About Jazz review, Samuel Chell describes two quartets, both with drummer Billy Higgins. One has pianist Stanley Cowell and bassist Bill Lee, while the other has Cedar Walton and Sam Jones.[3] These are different bands from the Jazz Showcase quartet, so the comparison gives you another setting for Jordan's playing.

I would begin with "Cal Massey," which appears in the published listings for both albums.[2][3] Chell discusses it as Jordan's musical portrait of the composer and trumpeter, and pays particular attention to how Cowell continues the ideas of the saxophone solo.[3] That supplies a specific question for the studio recording. What happens to the musical idea when the next player takes over?

If you already know *Glass Bead Games*, the later live recording offers a way to revisit a familiar title with different musicians. If you do not, the studio album is worth investigating in its own right. It is not homework you must finish to qualify for the new release.

Chell's review is useful criticism, not a current buying guide. I would not carry its comments about expensive copies or reissue availability into a shopping decision in 2026. Use the musical observations and check today's editions separately.

## Hear the club as part of the history

The venue name deserves more attention than a line on the cover. The National Endowment for the Arts traces Joe Segal's jazz presenting in Chicago to 1947, including afternoon sessions at Roosevelt University. After those sessions ended in 1957, he continued at other locations before opening the first official Jazz Showcase on Rush Street in the early 1970s.[4]

That history keeps me from imagining one unchanged room behind every record carrying the club's name. The NEA account describes changing locations and a continuing commitment to presenting musicians. It also identifies Segal as a 2015 NEA Jazz Master recognized for jazz advocacy.[4]

For this listening session, I would keep the band and the engagement date beside the album title. "Chicago, 1976" is a more useful starting point than a vague idea of a lost jazz era. The recording documents particular musicians working together, not every version of the club or Jordan's career.

## Keep an archive's history separate from its sound

There is independent coverage of the wider Jazz Showcase series. In April, Jeff Wilson reviewed the earlier Joe Henderson, Ahmad Jamal, Yusef Lateef, and Mal Waldron releases for The Absolute Sound.[5] His comments belong to those records. They cannot establish how the forthcoming Jordan LP sounds.

What I find useful is the specificity of his listening. Wilson discusses whether bass stays audible, drums sound convincing, and instruments disappear in the mix.[5] Those are things a listener can check. A general promise of archival importance cannot answer them.

I would take those questions into the "Confirmation" preview, then reserve judgment on the physical edition. An advance digital track does not demonstrate how a particular LP copy tracks or how quiet its surfaces are. Nor does the announced record weight answer either question.

Documentation can give a recording historical interest before anyone reviews the pressing. That is a reason to investigate it, not a substitute for deciding whether I want to hear the performance again.

## Check which edition you are actually considering

The official digital listing includes tracks explicitly marked as trio performances, and its credits identify the last four tracks as trio only.[2] That is useful information about the digital program. It is not a verified map of the double LP's sides.

Before ordering, check the track list for the exact format being sold. Do not assume every item displayed on a digital album page appears on the vinyl edition in the same sequence. If the shop cannot confirm the LP contents yet, leave that question open rather than fill in the gaps yourself.

The later digital release also gives you another option. Someone who wants to hear the complete digital program before buying can wait until December 4.[1][2] Missing the first day of vinyl sales and missing the music are different things.

## Make one listening decision before a buying decision

Here is the sequence I would use this week:

1. Open the official album page and play the available "Confirmation" preview. Note the four musicians before listening.
2. Replay it while following piano, bass, or drums instead of the tenor. Write down what you want to hear again.
3. Find a licensed version of *Glass Bead Games* and try "Cal Massey." Follow the handoff between Jordan and the rhythm section described in Chell's review.[3]
4. Decide whether your interest is in Jordan generally, this particular quartet, or both. An earlier album may answer your curiosity without a new purchase.
5. If you want the LP, ask a participating shop about the exact edition and its contents. The label identifies the vinyl as a Black Friday release through participating independent retailers.[2]

If other unplayed records already need an evening, add this investigation to your [vinyl listening queue](/blog/2026/08/build-vinyl-listening-queue/) rather than immediately adding another order.

I started with an unheard tape and a name worth investigating. Tonight, play "Confirmation" twice and choose one musician to follow on the second pass. Whether you eventually buy *Let It Ring* should depend on wanting to return to that music, not merely on learning that it was hidden for years.
