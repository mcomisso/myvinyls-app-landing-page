A box of singles is easy to lose control of. The sleeves are thin, the writing on the spine is often nonexistent, and two copies of the same song may have different B-sides. Filing them with LPs usually leaves the little records leaning against much larger jackets.

Give the singles their own upright box and a rule you can remember. This is a filing system you can put together in one afternoon, without turning listening into a database project.

## Start by separating formats, not artists

Sort by diameter first. A 7-inch record is not necessarily a 45: the International Association of Sound and Audiovisual Archives notes that 7-inch singles and EPs were also made for 33⅓ rpm playback.[1] The University of Illinois preservation guide also lists 7-inch shellac discs among older formats.[4] If a disc is unusually heavy, rigid, cracked, or otherwise unfamiliar, set it aside rather than assuming it belongs with modern vinyl singles.

Make three temporary piles: ordinary 7-inch vinyl, discs needing identification, and anything needing care because the sleeve is torn or the disc is dirty. Read the speed printed on each label before filing; do not infer it solely from the diameter or the size of the centre hole. If the label gives no clear answer, research that exact edition before playing it.

## Pair every disc with its paper trail

A printed company sleeve or picture sleeve is part of the object, not disposable wrapping. IASA cautions that original packaging can carry information, and warns against separating a recording from its documentation without careful cross-referencing.[3]

Work one single at a time. Compare the artist, song, label, and catalogue number on both sides with the outer sleeve. For a plain company sleeve, check the label and any writing before deciding it belongs to that disc. Put uncertain matches in a separate "research" divider. Do not write a guessed pairing onto an original sleeve.

If an original sleeve sheds paper dust or rubs against the grooves, put the disc in a clean, correctly sized protective sleeve. Keep the original beside it in the same outer enclosure, or give both the same identifier so they cannot drift apart. IASA names polyethylene and acid-free paper as suitable replacement materials for vinyl-disc enclosures; it also warns that some old PVC sleeves can damage record surfaces.[3]

## File upright in short runs

The good storage rule is almost boring: upright, supported, and not crushed. IASA explicitly includes 7-inch singles in its shelf-load guidance and says discs should stand vertically with separators about half a disc's diameter apart. The records should not be tightly packed or left leaning for long periods.[2]

Use a box or shelf sized for singles, with sturdy dividers. Keep the run loose enough to pull one sleeve out without dragging its neighbours. A long, partly empty box needs more dividers, not a stack of singles laid flat at one end. Store the box away from damp, strong heat, and direct sun; the Illinois preservation guide identifies contamination, warping, and groove wear as risks across grooved-disc formats.[4]

My default filing rule is artist surname, then song title. It is fast at the shop and at home. If you mainly use singles for DJ sets, genre or set order may suit you better. Either way, write the filing key on a new divider or outer enclosure, not on the label or original sleeve.

## Record both sides, not just the hit

For each single, note:

- artist and both side titles, including their A/B or other printed side designations
- label and catalogue number exactly as printed
- stated speed, disc size, and any unusual centre-hole arrangement
- sleeve type and whether it is the original matching picture sleeve
- condition of the disc and sleeve separately
- its physical box and divider location

This is especially useful when one song appears on several singles. A title search may find the song, but the B-side, label, and number tell you which object you have. If the printing conflicts or the sleeve is unverified, write "unconfirmed" rather than resolving the ambiguity from memory. Photograph both labels and the sleeve before you reorganize a large pile.

## Make the first play a check, not a test of luck

Take out one record by its edges, avoiding the grooves. The Illinois guide recommends handling discs at roughly the 3 and 9 o'clock positions and returning them to an enclosure after use.[4] Inspect the disc under light for cracks and debris. Leave a suspect disc out of the listening queue until you know what it is.

Check the speed on the label, select that speed on the turntable, and use an adapter if a large centre hole needs one. Make sure the disc sits flat and centred before cueing. Use a normal microgroove stylus for a known modern vinyl single; a questionable old coarse-groove disc needs separate identification and appropriate playback equipment. The Illinois preservation guide warns that grooved discs need a suitable speed and stylus for their format.[4]

After playing, put the record back with its own sleeve, in its own divider. That small habit does more for a singles box than any elaborate sorting scheme.

## A box you can actually use

Try the system on twenty records first. Label a few dividers, identify both sides, isolate uncertain sleeves, and leave space for new finds. If you cannot locate a particular B-side quickly, change the filing key before you process the rest.

The goal is not a perfect inventory. It is knowing where a small record lives, which sleeve belongs to it, and how to play it without guessing.
