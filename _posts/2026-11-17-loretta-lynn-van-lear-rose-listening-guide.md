A famous duet can make an album seem familiar before you know the other songs. With Loretta Lynn's *Van Lear Rose*, "Portland Oregon" and producer Jack White offer an obvious point of entry. I would use them as the beginning of a listening session, then follow Lynn through the rest of the record.

Third Man scheduled its expanded edition for November 13, 2026, on vinyl, CD, and digital formats.[1] This week's reissue gives us a reason to return to the 2004 album, but the useful question is musical. What changes when Lynn moves between family memory, a character's predicament, and her own life?

You do not need a new pressing to investigate that. This is a guide to the songs and their different perspectives, not a sound-quality review of the reissue.

## Separate the new edition from a new discovery

The expanded edition adds "Just to Have You Back" after "Story of My Life." Third Man's announcement lists fourteen tracks, with the original title song still first and "Portland Oregon" second.[1] The addition comes after the earlier album's ending, which matters if you want to understand its original sequence.

Do not mistake that bonus for a recording nobody has heard before. The label's product page says it was previously available through its Vault subscription series.[2] Pitchfork's reissue report identifies that earlier edition as a 2015 release.[3] For a listener without that version, it may be new to the collection without being newly unearthed.

I would hear the original sequence through "Story of My Life" before treating the extra song as part of the same ending. That is a listening choice, not a claim that the expanded running order is wrong. It lets you notice what the addition changes instead of assuming a longer edition tells exactly the same story.

## Start with the parents, not the producer

The title track offers a useful correction to the temptation to make this White's story. In his April 29, 2004 Pitchfork review, Stephen M. Deusner describes Lynn recalling her father's stories about her mother, including the miners' doubts that he could win her affection.[4] The family relationship is the subject before the famous collaborator gets his duet.

For a first listen, follow who is speaking and who is being described. A song can feel intimate without every event happening directly to its singer. Here, the remembered account of her parents gives you a different kind of autobiography from a straightforward list of events in Lynn's own career.

Then notice how the accompaniment supports the telling. Does an instrumental phrase pull your attention away from a sentence, or give you time to absorb it? I would keep that question open rather than decide in advance that rougher production must be more truthful. The words need room in the listening session too.

## Hear what the band contributes

Deusner describes a backing group with Jack Lawrence and Patrick Keeler from the Greenhornes, plus Blanche's Dave Feeny on pedal steel and slide guitar. He hears a band able to support a country setting while also making rock noise.[4] Those are useful credits to carry into the duet, rather than hearing every instrumental decision as White alone.

His review points to the blues riffs and exchanged verses of "Portland Oregon," then the barroom energy of "Mrs. Leroy Brown."[4] Use those tracks to compare how the band works around Lynn. Follow the rhythm section for one passage, then replay it with your attention on the vocal phrasing.

I would resist making that comparison a contest between country and rock. Ask a smaller question that you can answer from the performance. Does the accompaniment leave a line clear, answer it, or compete with it? Naming the moment is more useful than deciding which genre won the collaboration.

## Distinguish a memory from a character song

The album's changes of perspective are easy to miss if you concentrate only on its production. Deusner identifies "Little Red Shoes" as a spoken reminiscence and "Miss Being Mrs." as a widow's lament. He discusses "Women's Prison" and "Family Tree" alongside the subjects of Lynn's earlier songs.[4] These do not all ask for the same kind of attention.

With "Little Red Shoes," follow the account before evaluating it as a conventional song. With "Miss Being Mrs.," listen to the way an absence becomes the subject. For the character songs, establish the situation the lyric gives you instead of assuming the narrator and the performer are interchangeable.

Finally, give "Story of My Life" its full place in the sequence. Deusner describes its account of marriage, motherhood, and stardom, and hears satisfaction rather than a closing inventory of hardship.[4] After the album's other situations, that direct life summary gives you something specific to compare with the opening family story.

## Try one uninterrupted listening session

MusicRow's reissue coverage prints the running order, including "Little Red Shoes" seventh, "Miss Being Mrs." twelfth, and "Story of My Life" thirteenth.[5] Use that list to find your reference points. For a physical copy, follow the side divisions printed on your edition rather than infer them from a digital tracklist.

Here is the session I would try:

1. Begin with "Van Lear Rose." Identify the family relationship at the center of the song before concentrating on the instruments.
2. Let "Portland Oregon" play in sequence. Notice what changes when another lead voice enters the album, then continue rather than replay the duet immediately.
3. At "Little Red Shoes," follow the spoken account. Compare that way of telling a memory with the opening song.
4. Give "Miss Being Mrs." and "Story of My Life" separate attention near the end. Write one sentence about how their subjects differ.
5. Pause after "Story of My Life" if your edition allows it. Then hear "Just to Have You Back" as the addition and decide whether you want it to follow the earlier ending on your next listen.

Keep the notes short enough that they do not interrupt the music. If one passage prompts a question, save it for a replay after the album. You are trying to hear the sequence before turning it into a set of isolated examples.

## Decide about the record after the songs

The label lists standard black and limited-edition orange vinyl alongside CD.[2] Those options tell you how the edition is offered. They do not establish which pressing sounds better, and I have not auditioned either vinyl version.

If you already own a playable copy, start there. The extra track is a concrete difference to weigh after the listening session, not an automatic reason to replace your record. If you are new to the album, choose the format only after you know whether the songs hold your attention beyond the duet.

For another album approached beyond its best-known opening, try the Journal's [guide to Minnie Riperton's Come to My Garden](/blog/2026/10/minnie-riperton-come-to-my-garden/). The musical setting is different, but it offers another way to give the less familiar songs a fair hearing.

Before putting *Van Lear Rose* away, choose one song other than "Portland Oregon" to replay. Name the detail you want to check, whether it is a remembered conversation or a change in the accompaniment. That gives your next listen a purpose beyond recognizing the famous partnership.
