A replacement stylus can look like the smallest purchase in a turntable setup and still be the wrong part. Before I would order one, I would want the model of the cartridge actually mounted on the arm. A familiar turntable name or a matching plastic color would not be enough.

November's What Hi-Fi? Awards coverage puts turntable upgrades back in view, including new cartridge and tonearm combinations.[6] My starting point is less expensive. If the existing cartridge accepts a replacement stylus, establish exactly which assembly belongs in it before deciding that maintenance requires a whole new cartridge. This guide is about that compatibility check, not a ranking of upgrades.

## Identify the cartridge that is there now

The headshell is the support at the end of the tonearm. The cartridge mounts beneath it, and a replaceable stylus assembly fits into the cartridge on designs that allow it. Audio-Technica's VM95 instructions show these as separate parts, with the cartridge staying attached to the headshell during a stylus change.[5]

Write down the turntable model, then inspect the cartridge for its own model marking. Take a clear photograph without touching the cantilever or diamond. Compare that photograph and identifier with the manufacturer's documentation, rather than assuming the cartridge is whatever originally shipped with the turntable.

I would treat an unreadable or missing identifier as a reason to pause. A secondhand player may have a different cartridge from the factory specification. Send a dealer or the manufacturer photographs of the fitted part and ask for a confirmed replacement code before buying.

Keep the cartridge name and stylus name separate in your notes. For example, Audio-Technica lists AT-VM95E as the cartridge and AT-VMN95E as its replacement stylus.[1] That extra letter is part of the identifier, not a spelling variation to ignore in a listing.

## Separate physical fit from the intended combination

Some cartridge families make replacement straightforward. Audio-Technica states that the VM95 cartridges share the same body and accept the six AT-VMN95 replacement styli.[1] That is a documented relationship within a named family. It does not establish compatibility with every cartridge carrying the same manufacturer's name.

Ortofon's 2M guidance adds a useful distinction. The 2M Red and Blue share a generator, while the Bronze, Black, and Black LVB 250 form another generator group. Ortofon recommends upgrades within those groups. It also says a Black stylus physically fits a Red body but does not turn it into a complete Black cartridge because the generator differs.[2]

The practical question is therefore more precise than whether a part slides into place. Does the manufacturer list it for this body, and is it the intended combination for the result you want? I would not pay for a higher-priced stylus on the assumption that every part left behind becomes equivalent to the more expensive cartridge.

For a like-for-like repair, order the confirmed replacement code. For an upgrade, ask what changes with the stylus and what stays in the cartridge body. Those are different purchases even when fitting them involves the same movement.

## Know when a stylus swap is not the repair

A replaceable stylus is not a universal cartridge feature. What Hi-Fi?'s fitting guide explains that moving-magnet designs usually allow a replacement stylus assembly, while damage to a moving-coil cartridge can require replacement or specialist repair.[3] Do not try to pull apart an unfamiliar cartridge because a video shows another model coming apart.

Older equipment can introduce a different problem. Hi-Fi News' Sony PS-Q7 buying advice distinguishes the replaceable ND-143G stylus from the player's special cartridge. The review warns buyers to make sure that cartridge is present.[4] A stylus listing cannot solve a missing cartridge body.

That is a reason to identify what is absent before ordering for an inherited or incomplete player. Ask whether you need a stylus assembly, a complete cartridge, or a service assessment. If the body, mounting, or connections are damaged, get advice on those faults rather than treating a new needle as a complete repair.

## Use a compatibility checklist before opening the box

I would finish these checks while the old assembly is still in place. They separate the ordering decision from the delicate work of fitting the part. You should be able to complete the first four without dismantling anything.

1. Record the cartridge's full model identifier and photograph its position on the headshell.
2. Find the manufacturer's replacement-stylus code for that exact cartridge. Save the documentation with the order details.
3. If changing stylus type, check the manufacturer's compatibility guidance and the new assembly's setup requirements. Do not rely on color or a similar-looking housing.
4. Read the installation instructions for your cartridge and check whether your headshell detaches. Do not copy a detachable-headshell procedure onto an arm that uses a fixed headshell.
5. Stop playback, switch off the turntable, secure the arm, and follow the model-specific instructions. Handle the assembly by the housing where directed, not by its cantilever.
6. Before playing a record, confirm that the assembly is seated as instructed and check the specified tracking force.

Audio-Technica's VM95 procedure starts with securing the arm and using the stylus protector if available. It then removes the detachable headshell, handles the stylus assembly by its sides, and aligns the new assembly's tab with the cartridge body's opening.[5] Those details are useful because they describe one mechanism. They are not instructions to pull every stylus in the same direction.

## Check settings without creating another adjustment job

A stylus-only replacement and a complete cartridge installation are different jobs. In the VM95 procedure, the mounting screws and cartridge wires stay in place. Audio-Technica says rebalancing or resetting tracking force should not be necessary if the counterweight has not moved, while allowing for a double-check.[5]

I would still verify the applicable specification, especially when changing stylus type. The AT-VM95E specification gives a tracking-force range of 1.8 to 2.2 grams, with 2.0 grams standard.[1] That is an example for this model, not a setting to copy onto an unidentified cartridge.

Replacing the whole cartridge adds mounting, wiring, alignment, and tracking-force work. What Hi-Fi?'s guide describes using an alignment gauge and a tracking-force gauge, and warns that the cartridge leads are fragile.[3] If that is the job you actually need, budget for the tools or ask a technician to fit it.

For ongoing care after installation, the Journal's [guide to cleaning a turntable stylus safely](/blog/2026/10/clean-turntable-stylus-safely/) covers a separate task. Cleaning instructions do not establish replacement compatibility, and a new stylus does not remove the need to follow its care instructions.

## Order the part you can identify

There is no need to turn a maintenance purchase into an upgrade decision before you know what is fitted. A confirmed like-for-like replacement gives you a specific part to order and a specific installation procedure to follow. A different stylus needs its own compatibility and setup check.

Before putting anything in a cart, photograph the cartridge and write down its full identifier. Find the matching replacement code in the manufacturer's documentation. If either identifier remains uncertain, resolve that first rather than opening a package and trying the fit.
