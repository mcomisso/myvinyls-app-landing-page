The useful surprise in Albert Ayler's *Prophecy* is how much a simple tune can matter inside such unruly music. Mark Richardson's Pitchfork review describes melodies that stay in your head, followed by improvisation that pulls them apart.[3] I would start by remembering one of those tunes, rather than trying to understand every saxophone sound at once.

There is a timely reason to try. ORG Music has announced *Prophecy Completed*, a double LP for Record Store Day Black Friday on November 27, 2026.[1] Before treating the expanded edition as a collecting obligation, hear how Ayler, bassist Gary Peacock, and drummer Sunny Murray work through "Ghosts." The melody gives a first listen something specific to follow.

## Know what the expanded edition adds

The performance took place at New York's Cellar Cafe on June 14, 1964. Kyle Simpler's November 2026 All About Jazz preview identifies the trio and explains that *Prophecy Completed* expands the original 1975 album to the full concert.[2] This is an older performance returning in a different physical edition, not a newly recorded album.

Nor is all the additional music unheard. Richardson's January 2016 review of *Bells/Prophecy* explains that the original *Prophecy* contained five pieces. The remaining music from the evening had appeared on the *Holy Ghost* box in 2004 and was included in that expanded CD release.[3] The current announcement's first-time claim concerns the complete performance on vinyl.[1]

That distinction matters if you already own an expanded edition. Compare its contents before buying another copy for access to supposedly unknown music. For a newcomer, it also removes the pressure to begin with the forthcoming package. An existing authorized edition can introduce the trio before you decide whether you want two LPs.

## Keep the opening melody in mind

"Ghosts" offers a manageable first task. Richardson describes its jaunty opening theme and the trio's repeated transformations of it, with both a first and second variation on *Prophecy*.[3] The recurring composition lets you compare performances without having to learn a new tune each time.

Listen to the opening without taking notes. When the playing becomes harder to follow, recall the shape of that first melody. You do not need to identify a key or count bars. Ask whether you can still recognize a fragment, and notice the point at which recognition stops being easy.

Then wait for the theme to return. Richardson singles out the restatement near the end of "Ghosts, Second Variation," just before the audience's applause.[3] That return supplies a reason to stay through a passage that initially sounds impenetrable. You can hear how your response to a familiar tune changes after the improvisation.

I would resist calling every harsh sound a departure from the music. Richardson connects Ayler's wide vibrato, powerful low register, and high-register playing to the record's expressive character.[3] The tone is part of the performance to consider, rather than interference to mentally remove before getting to the composition.

## Hear Murray without demanding a steady beat

A familiar tune does not mean a familiar accompaniment. In his 2017 Guardian obituary, John Fordham describes Murray's development away from conventional timekeeping through his work with Cecil Taylor. He also emphasizes Murray's independence from the role of a drummer simply accompanying a soloist.[5]

That history suggests a useful adjustment. If you keep waiting for a regular ride-cymbal pattern to confirm where you are, try following changes in density instead. Notice when the percussion becomes busier, when it leaves more space, and whether those changes coincide with Ayler's phrases.

Richardson describes Murray's cymbal-heavy playing on these recordings as texture rather than conventional timekeeping. He also notes Peacock's alternation between bowed lines and a more pointed single-note attack.[3] Those observations give each player a separate listening task. Follow the cymbals for one passage, then return to the same passage for the bass.

Do not force yourself to hear a balanced conversation if the recording obscures one participant. That can be a limitation of what reaches your speakers, rather than a failure to pay attention.

## Compare the two Ghosts performances

For this exercise, use the two variations identified on the original album, rather than assume every expanded edition uses identical track titles. Keep the volume comfortable and the track list nearby. The purpose is to recognize a change in the playing, not complete the whole concert in one sitting.

1. Play the first variation through once. Remember the opening melody before trying to separate the instruments.
2. Play the second variation. Listen for what feels different in the theme statement and the point where the performance becomes less predictable.
3. Replay one passage with Murray in mind. Notice the cymbal sound and changes in percussion activity instead of looking for a constant beat.
4. Return to that passage for Peacock. If you cannot follow the bass, record that limitation rather than invent a description of it.
5. Stay through the ending of the second variation. Decide whether the returning tune sounds familiar in the same way it did at the beginning.

Write one sentence about the difference you heard. "I could follow the melody longer in the first version" is enough. A concrete observation is more useful than deciding whether you now understand free jazz.

## Separate the performance from the recording

The independent reviews do not describe a pristine listening experience. Reviewing the 2025 *Prophecy Live, First Visit* edition for All About Jazz, Glenn Astarita reports a difficult balance, with Murray sometimes dominating and Peacock harder to hear. He values the historical document while remaining critical of its sound.[4]

Richardson's earlier review is more enthusiastic about the trio's musical impact.[3] These judgments need not cancel each other out. A performance can reward close attention while its recording prevents you from hearing everything you want.

Neither review is an audition of the forthcoming ORG Music pressing. The label announces new remastering by Dave Gardner, but that credit alone does not establish how the new LP will sound in your room.[1] I have not played the pressing and would not promise that it solves the balance problems reported on another edition.

For another example of setting expectations around a live archive, the Journal's [guide to John Coltrane's Tiberi Tapes](/blog/2026/11/coltrane-tiberi-tapes-listening-guide/) separates musical interest from recording limitations. Apply that distinction here without assuming the two recordings share the same faults.

## Decide whether you want another listen

The expanded format is useful if you want more of this particular trio on this particular night. It is less useful if you are buying a document you feel obliged to admire. The announcement can establish the edition's contents, but the listening decision still belongs to you.

Start with the two "Ghosts" variations before making a shopping list. If the returning melody makes you want to hear how the trio reached it, continue with the concert. If the sound or intensity makes listening unpleasant, stop there for now. The next action is one comparison, not a commitment to a complete archive.
