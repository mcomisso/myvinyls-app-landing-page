A welcoming first song can set the wrong expectation for the rest of an album. With Joni Mitchell's *Night Ride Home*, I would resist treating the title as a promise of uninterrupted comfort. Its two LP sides give you a way to hear how the songs change the mood.

November's vinyl reissue offers a reason to make that comparison. Michael Fremer's October report for Tracking Angle gave November 13 as the shipping date and identified the album as Mitchell's 1991 release.[1] The useful question now is what deserves your attention after the opening song, whether you buy this edition or play a copy you already have.

This is a listening plan, not a review of the new pressing. I have not auditioned it, and the recommendations below do not depend on a claim that a particular edition sounds best.

## Separate the pressing from the songs

Interscope lists the reissue as an all-analog LP, mastered from the original analog master tapes by Chris Bellman at Bernie Grundman Mastering. It credits plating and pressing to Record Technologies, Inc. The published running order has five tracks on each side, beginning with "Night Ride Home" and ending with "Two Grey Rooms."[2]

Those are concrete details about the edition. They do not tell you how strongly you will respond to the songs, or establish a sound-quality ranking against every earlier copy. I would settle the musical question before making the mastering credits the reason to buy.

Fremer reported hearing a test pressing and preferred it to his original.[1] That is his comparison, with the limits of a pre-release report. It is useful context for someone researching the pressing, but I would not turn it into my own listening verdict or a guarantee about the finished copy you receive.

## Let the opening establish a place

In Pitchfork's career guide, Mark Richardson describes the title song's unhurried rhythm, cricket sounds, and relaxed vocal. He hears Mitchell concentrating on small roadside details during a trip with Larry Klein in Hawaii.[3] That gives you something more specific to follow than a general impression of acoustic warmth.

Rolling Stone's account also connects the song to a Fourth of July drive in Hawaii. It notes that Mitchell had performed it as "Fourth of July" before the album's release.[5] The setting is a particular remembered journey, not an instruction to hear every subsequent song as another peaceful evening.

On the first play, I would follow the scene before listening for individual instruments. Notice which details make the journey feel immediate. On a replay, give the pulse your attention and ask how much of that sense of ease comes from the accompaniment rather than the subject alone.

## Do not leave side A in the background

The reissue's first side moves through "Passion Play," "Cherokee Louise," and "The Windfall" before closing with "Slouching Towards Bethlehem."[2] That sequence is a reason to keep listening after the title song instead of letting its mood stand for the whole record.

Les Berkley's June 1991 Stereophile review describes "Cherokee Louise" as a childhood-friendship story with abuse beneath its understated telling. He identifies "Slouching Towards Bethlehem" as Mitchell's setting of W. B. Yeats's "The Second Coming."[4] Both ask for attention to the words, though they approach very different subjects.

I would avoid trying to make those songs agree emotionally with the opener. Follow the relationship in "Cherokee Louise" and the images in "Slouching Towards Bethlehem" on their own terms. A restrained performance does not make its subject mild, and a comfortable listening volume does not turn the lyrics into background material.

At the end of the side, pause long enough to name what changed. You do not need to interpret the whole Yeats poem before turning the record. One observation about the distance between the opening scene and the closing song gives you something to test on the next play.

## Give side B its own beginning

"Come in from the Cold" opens the second side.[2] Fremer singles it out as a highlight lasting more than seven minutes.[1] Treating it as a new beginning, after the physical pause to turn the LP, gives that length a purpose in the listening session rather than making it another track in a continuous stream.

Pitchfork's Tyler Wilcox follows the song from adolescence into adult accomplishments and disappointments. He argues that it offers no tidy resolution, while its steady backing marks the passage of time.[3] I would listen for the changes in perspective across the verses instead of waiting only for the refrain to return.

The ending deserves different attention. Berkley praises the opening piano figure in "Two Grey Rooms," even though he is less persuaded by "Nothing Can Be Done" and "Ray's Dad's Cadillac."[4] His mixed response is a useful reminder that rediscovering an album does not require liking every song equally.

Leave the final track uninterrupted, then compare the kind of attention it asked of you with the title song. If one holds you more strongly, keep that preference. There is no need to explain it away to make the album seem uniformly successful.

## Try a two-side listening session

Use the side divisions printed on your own copy. For the new edition, Interscope's sequence places "Slouching Towards Bethlehem" at the end of side A and "Come in from the Cold" at the start of side B.[2] A digital listen can follow the same break without pretending to test the vinyl mastering.

1. Play side A without rearranging the tracks. Let the title song establish its setting, then follow where the remaining songs take your attention.
2. Stop after "Slouching Towards Bethlehem." Write one sentence about how the side's ending differs from its beginning.
3. Turn the record and hear "Come in from the Cold" in full. Follow the changing life stages rather than counting how soon the refrain returns.
4. Continue through "Two Grey Rooms." Wait until it finishes before choosing a favorite or starting a replay.
5. Return to one passage that changed your impression of the album. Note the song and the detail you want to hear again, not a verdict on the whole pressing.

I would keep the written part to those few notes. If you spend the session trying to document every line, the procedure starts interrupting the music it was meant to help you hear.

## Choose the return listen before the purchase

For another approach to a songwriter's changing perspectives, try the Journal's [guide to Loretta Lynn's Van Lear Rose](/blog/2026/11/loretta-lynn-van-lear-rose-listening-guide/). That album has a different band and setting, but it also rewards attention beyond its best-known point of entry.

With *Night Ride Home*, the next step is smaller than deciding whether the reissue is essential. Choose either the turn between the sides or the contrast between the first and last songs, then listen again with that comparison in mind. You will have a reason to return to the record that goes beyond the appeal of its title or its mastering credits.
